<div style="font-weight: normal; font-style: normal; font-size: 16px; color: rgb(85, 85, 85);"><span style="font-weight: bolder; font-style: normal; font-size: 16px;">Love is in the water</span>
<div style="text-align: left;">Gorgeous <a href="https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=k1yu9S3u9lc">drone footage</a> taken off California’s southern coast this month gives us an intimate insight into the lives of lovestruck whales. While two migrating grey whales indulge in a little flirting, a pod of dolphins excitedly flip and jump alongside them. The whales appear to join in the fun, splashing their giant 30-ton bodies around in the water.<img src="/images/letters/inferred-2018-01-31-2-1-docx-templateword-a24a88e444b418b8.jpg" style="width: 343px; max-width: 343px;display:block;float:none;clear:both;height:auto;max-width:100%;margin:0.75rem auto;" width="343"/>It is thought that the area is a waypoint on the whales’ 12,000 mile round trip from Alaska to the warmer waters of Mexico. Once they arrive in Mexico, they mate and give birth, so it’s only natural that there’s a little courting on the way down.<br/>
<br/>
In Australia, scientists have observed dolphins <a href="https://video.nationalgeographic.com/video/news/171020-male-humpback-dolphins-sponges-love-token-vin-spd">giving sponges to potential mates</a> in a possible show of courtship. </div>
<hr/><span style="font-weight: bolder; font-style: normal; font-size: 16px;">Vaquita</span><br/>
The world’s smallest porpoise, the Vaquita, faces risk of extinction, with only around 30 individuals remaining. Vaquitas routinely drown after getting caught up in gillnets used in the shrimp fisheries of the upper Gulf of California.<br/>
The Government of Mexico has now <a href="https://edition.cnn.com/2017/07/02/americas/mexico-bans-gill-nets-vaquita-porpoise/index.html">banned</a> the nets, which are largely invisible to the porpoises, and an international committee of gear experts is now tackling the issue too. A new type of <a href="http://www.cbc.ca/news/canada/newfoundland-labrador/marine-institute-flume-tank-endangered-vaquitas-1.4504090">porpoise-friendly net</a> is currently being tested at the Centre for Sustainable Aquatic Resources in Canada. There is therefore hope that the remaining Vaquitas will get a reprieve.

<hr/>
<div style="font-style: normal; font-size: 16px;"><span style="font-weight: bolder; font-style: normal; font-size: 16px;">Gif of the week</span></div>
<img src="/images/letters/giphy-dFwQUP8nPO7ni.gif" style="width: 400px; max-width: 400px;display:block;float:none;clear:both;height:auto;max-width:100%;margin:0.75rem auto;" width="400"/>Dolphins are born “feet first”. Cetaceans are unable to breathe underwater, so if the baby’s head came first, it would suffocate during the birth. As soon as the umbilical cord is severed, the baby dolphin instinctively rising to the surface to draw breath.<br/>
<br/>
The little dolphin is born without lips, which makes suckling a challenge. The baby dolphin lightly rattles his ribs against the mother's belly, letting her know he’s hungry and stimulating milk production. The mother then sprays the milk directly into the baby’s mouth.
<hr/><span style="font-weight: bolder; font-size: 16px;">#OceanOptimism</span><br/>
A new tribe of dolphins has been identified <a href="https://www.independent.co.uk/news/uk/home-news/bottlenose-dolphins-english-coast-discovery-cornwall-devon-st-ives-coast-sea-a8118496.html">off the English coast</a>. The Cornwall Wildlife Trust has spotted 28 individual bottlenose dolphins living near St Ives (with occasional forays to Devon and Dorset). Meanwhile, anecdotal reports of increased numbers of humpback whales off the southern and western coasts of Ireland are matched by an upward global trend for this species - a vindication of the 1983 global moratorium on the hunting of great whales.

<hr/><strong>Speaking in tongues</strong><br/>
In Crimea, a captive beluga whale has seemingly <a href="https://www.sciencealert.com/beluga-whale-bottlenose-dolphin-learns-new-language">"learned" a foreign language</a>. Two months after arriving at the dolphinarium, the whale stopped making its usual vocalisations and started making the whistling tones unique to dolphins. Researchers have previously observed the same linguistic ability in killer whales, and last week <a href="https://www.theguardian.com/science/2018/jan/31/orcas-killer-whales-can-imitate-human-speech-research-reveals">new research revealed</a> that orcas can even mimic human words such as ‘hello’ and ‘bye bye’.
