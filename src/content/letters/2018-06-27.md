<div style="font-weight: 400; font-style: normal; font-size: 16px; color: rgb(85, 85, 85);"><strong>Sustainable development – are we nearly there yet?</strong><br/>
Last week the UN published its <a href="https://unstats.un.org/sdgs/report/2018/overview/">annual report on the Sustainable Development Goals</a> (SDGs). The good news for the ocean goal (SDG14) is that countries have doubled the coverage of <a href="http://www.mpatlas.org/map/mpas/">protected areas</a> in their waters over the past decade. But the stocktake for is still worrying: declining marine fish stocks, deterioration of coastal waters due to pollution and <a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eutrophication">eutrophication</a>. The report calls for more ambitious political commitments, massive financial investment, and development of innovative new technologies.<br/>

<hr/><strong>OOO! (Observe Our Ocean!)</strong><br/>
A new <a href="http://www.jcommops.org/reportcard2018/">Ocean Observing System report card</a> provides a snapshot of ocean observing systems. <span style="color: rgb(85, 85, 85); font-size: 16px; font-style: normal; font-weight: 400;">We can’t manage and protect our ocean if we don’t know what’s going on, and these systems</span> provide data that is crucial for: predicting and managing extreme weather and coastal hazards; monitoring the state of the seas; and even tracking <a href="https://slideplayer.com/slide/6228521/">oil spills</a>. As a society, we are demanding ever more of such systems, yet finding sustained financing is a huge challenge. Not only must we maintain, update and strengthen existing systems, we also have gaps to fill in the polar regions, sea basins, and the deep ocean.<br/>

<hr/><strong>Sail the 7(0) seas</strong><br/>
Sailor Emily Penn, accompanied by an all-female crew, recently set off for the Great Pacific Garbage Patch to carry out <a href="https://www.bbc.com/news/science-environment-44579420">scientific experiments</a> concerning the effects of plastic on marine life.<br/>

<br/>
But how many oceans are there? There is, of course, just one global ocean. Historically, “The Seven Seas” has been used to refer to all the world’s waters (the term first known use is from a Sumerian hymn written in 2300 BC!). More recently we’ve named four distinct oceans: Atlantic, Pacific, Indian, and Arctic. The Southern Ocean around Antarctica is the newest to get a name (its boundaries were only proposed to the International Hydrographic Organization (IHO) in 2000). Meanwhile, the IHO maintains <a href="https://www.iho.int/mtg_docs/com_wg/S-23WG/S-23WG_Misc/Draft_2002/Draft_2002.htm">a list</a> of over 70 distinct bodies of water called seas.

<hr/><strong>From the editor</strong><br/>
<a href="https://www.un.org/bbnj/">Negotiations</a> for a new high seas treaty start in September, and there is plenty of pre-game chatter. Last week I was at<span style="color: rgb(85, 85, 85); font-size: 16px; font-style: normal; font-weight: 400;"> the Institut océanographique in Paris for</span> <em><a href="http://enb.iisd.org/sea/hsa/whats-at-stake/">The High Seas: What's at Stake?</a></em>, and this week at the Nausicaa Aquarium for another (photos of me enjoying their fantastic high seas exhibition below). My institute is looking for a new <a href="https://www.iddri.org/index.php/en/about-iddri/carreers/governance-programme-director">Governance Programme Director</a>. Donalda's note on ocean observing systems made me think of the latest episode of the <em>Freakonomics</em> podcast, <a href="http://freakonomics.com/podcast/in-praise-of-maintenance/">"In Praise of Maintenance"</a>. Sure, innovation is sexy, but maintenance is necessary. Should you pee on your leg if you are <a href="https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC4962017/">stung by jellyfish</a>?
