<div style="font-weight: 400; font-style: normal; font-size: 16px; color: rgb(85, 85, 85);"><span style="font-weight: bolder; font-size: 16px;">Party poopers</span><br/>
A <a href="https://www.hakaimagazine.com/news/gulls-aim-for-humans-when-they-poop/">parody article</a> published on April Fools day claimed that people with brown hair are particularly at risk of being dumped on by seagulls, while one <a href="http://www.businessinsider.com/halfords-study-finds-birds-poop-on-red-cars-the-most-2012-6">informal study</a> did actually find that seagulls may prefer to poop on red cars.<br/>
<br/>
All jokes aside, seagull poo can be a serious issue. In the US, it is thought that 1.4 million seagulls feed at landfills, dropping <a href="https://phys.org/news/2017-06-trash-picking-seagulls-poop-tons-nutrients.html">tons of nitrogen and phosphorus</a> into nearby waterways and contributing to algal blooms that suffocate marine life. In Canada, one port authority was sick of spending $20-30,000 a year cleaning up the guano (which stinks and is so acidic that it erodes roofs); they built a fake tree to attract nesting eagles that will <a href="http://toronto.citynews.ca/2018/03/28/b-c-port-hopes-to-reduce-gull-poop-by-attracting-eagles-to-fake-tree/">scare off the seagulls</a>. <br/>

<hr/><span style="font-weight: bolder; font-size: 16px;">Life in plastic</span><br/>
With an estimated 150 million tons of plastic in the oceans, there could soon be <a href="https://www.washingtonpost.com/news/morning-mix/wp/2016/01/20/by-2050-there-will-be-more-plastic-than-fish-in-the-worlds-oceans-study-says/">more plastic than fish</a>. This week, I learned that plastics <a href="http://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0191759">absorb and release toxic metals</a> into marine ecosystems. But there is always some cause for hope. Following the discovery last year of an enzyme naturally able to <a href="https://www.livescience.com/62328-plastic-eating-enzyme.html">break down some plastics</a>, scientists <a href="http://www.pnas.org/content/early/2018/04/16/1718804115">introduced a mutation</a> which speeds up the process. As always, further research is required, but the enzyme could one day contribute to the ocean cleanup.<br/>

<hr/><span style="font-weight: bolder; font-size: 16px;">A whale of a time</span><br/>
From the 6-10 April the European Cetacean Society (ECS) held its 32nd annual conference. This year's theme was "Marine Conservation - forging effective, strategic partnerships". Over 600 people from all over the world participated: check out the proceedings <a href="http://www.europeancetaceansociety.eu/sites/default/files/27253 ABSTRACT BOOK_LOW RES.pdf">here</a>.<br/>

<hr/><strong>From the editor</strong><br/>
Seagulls pooped on me a lot as a kid. I thought I was unlucky, but now I wonder if they target redheads? The extent of deep-sea bottom trawling appears to have been <a href="https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fmars.2018.00098/full">underestimated</a> (no surprise there). If you read this newsletter, you probably already know that doom and gloom is not a good advocacy strategy. A recent study showed that ocean news is <a href="https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0959378017311986">surprisingly upbeat</a>. I've been binge-listening to a new podcast called <em><a href="https://www.alieward.com/ologies/">Ologies</a>. </em>There are excellent episodes on <a href="https://www.alieward.com/ologies/2018/2/24/19-teuthology-with-sarah-mcanulty">teuthology</a> (the study of squids and such) and <a href="https://www.alieward.com/ologies/2018/1/29/16-ichthyology-with-chris-thacker">ichthyology</a> (the study of fish). Spring has finally sprung in Paris - bon weekend!
