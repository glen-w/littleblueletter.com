<hr/><span style="font-weight: bolder;">Spotlight on deep-sea mining</span>
<div dir="ltr">The International Seabed Authority (ISA) has been <a href="https://www.reuters.com/article/us-mining-deepsea/u-n-deep-sea-mining-body-rejects-greenpeace-criticism-idUSKCN1U016E">in the spotlight</a> recently as negotiations for a "Mining Code" to regulate deep-sea mining (DSM) continue. The ISA has <span style="font-size: 16px;">issued the 30th exploration licence to China (China's 5th). At present, multinational corporations, start-ups and state-backed companies conduct exploration activities that cover more than 1.3 million square kilometres of the seabed.<br/>
<img src="/images/letters/inferred-2019-10-11-2-1-amazonriverdolhpin.png" style="width: 640px; max-width: 640px;display:block;float:none;clear:both;height:auto;max-width:100%;margin:0.75rem auto;" width="640"/></span></div>
<div dir="ltr" style="text-align: center;"><em>Machines for mining seabed minerals made by Nautilus, whose failed project in Papua New Guinea has <a href="http://www.theguardian.com/world/2019/sep/16/collapse-of-png-deep-sea-mining-venture-sparks-calls-for-moratorium">cost the government $157 million</a>.</em></div>
<div dir="ltr"><br/>
<span style="font-size: 16px;">But concern is mounting as to whether DSM can ever be sustainable. </span>A couple of new reports make vociferous arguments against DSM<em style="font-weight: 400; font-size: 16px;">,</em><span style="font-weight: 400; font-style: normal; font-size: 16px;"> warning of the potential for irreversible harm and questioning the ISA's impartiality and approach to regulation</span>: <em style="font-size: 16px;"><a href="https://www.greenpeace.org/international/publication/22578/deep-sea-mining-in-deep-water/" style="font-size: 16px;">In Deep Water: the Emerging Threat of Deep Sea Mining</a></em> (Greenpeace) and <em style="font-size: 16px;"><a href="http://www.deepseaminingoutofourdepth.org/wp-content/uploads/Why-the-Rush.pdf" style="font-size: 16px;">Why the Rush? seabed mining in the Pacific ocean</a></em> (Deep Sea Mining Campaign).</div>
<div dir="ltr"><span style="font-size: 16px; font-style: normal; font-weight: 400;"><img src="/images/letters/inferred-2019-10-11-2-2-riverdolphin.jpeg" style="width: 446px; max-width: 446px;display:block;float:none;clear:both;height:auto;max-width:100%;margin:0.75rem auto;" width="446"/></span>
<div style="text-align: center;"><em>Giant tube worms, over three feet tall, live off the "smoke" particles from a hydrothermal vent. ©MBARI</em></div>
<br/>
<span style="font-size: 16px; font-style: normal; font-weight: 400;">Calls for a moratorium are also growing. The Deep Sea Conservation Coalition (DSCC) is the latest group to </span><a href="http://www.savethehighseas.org/wp-content/uploads/2019/08/DSCC-IGC3-release-f.pdf" style="font-size: 16px; font-style: normal; font-weight: 400; height: inherit;">call for a 10-year</a><span style="font-size: 16px; font-style: normal; font-weight: 400;"> pause and a group of 28 deep-sea scientists have </span><a href="https://storage.googleapis.com/planet4-international-stateless/2019/07/23dc9f03-isa-scientists-letter-2019.pdf" style="font-size: 16px; font-style: normal; font-weight: 400; height: inherit;">written to the ISA secretariat</a><span style="font-size: 16px; font-style: normal; font-weight: 400;"> warning that mining “puts the overall health of ocean ecosystems under threat”. Meanwhile Fiji has </span><a href="https://www.theguardian.com/world/2019/sep/16/collapse-of-png-deep-sea-mining-venture-sparks-calls-for-moratorium" style="font-size: 16px; font-style: normal; font-weight: 400; height: inherit;">urged its Pacific neighbours</a><span style="font-size: 16px; font-style: normal; font-weight: 400;"> to support a moratorium  to allow for scientific research on potential impacts (Vanuatu and Papua New Guinea expressed their support).</span>​

<hr/><strong>Ugly deep-sea creature of the week</strong>
<div style="text-align: center;"><em style="font-weight: 400; font-size: 16px; text-align: center;">“As someone fascinated by weird and wonderful wildlife, the deep sea is a dream come true. <a href="https://oceana.org/marine-life/ocean-fishes/stoplight-loosejaw" style="font-size: 16px;">Stoplight loosejaws</a>, bearded sea-devils and vampire squid are just a few of the fantastically named creatures that make the deep ocean their home.”- </em><span style="font-size: 16px; font-style: normal; font-weight: 400; text-align: center;">Chris Packham (</span><em style="font-weight: 400; font-size: 16px; text-align: center;">Blue Planet</em><span style="font-size: 16px; font-style: normal; font-weight: 400; text-align: center;">)</span></div>
<img src="/images/letters/inferred-2019-10-11-2-3-ehu54toxuaamxyg.jpeg" style="width: 491px; max-width: 491px;display:block;float:none;clear:both;height:auto;max-width:100%;margin:0.75rem auto;" width="491"/>
<p dir="ltr" style="text-align: center;"><em>"When I was filming Blue Planet Live, I was struck by just how much of the ocean has been altered by humans. From industrial fisheries ensnaring ocean giants in kilometres-long lines, to finding our trash at some of the deepest parts of the ocean: it’s clear that however vast the seas are, we are causing profound harm. Yet at this point in history, when the oceans are facing more pressures than ever before, a secretive new industry is seeking to move into the deep sea, the largest ecosystem on the planet, to start mining for metals and minerals.“</em></p>
